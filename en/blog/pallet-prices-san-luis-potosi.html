Wooden Pallet Prices in San Luis Potosí: What Drives the Cost

The price of a wooden pallet in San Luis Potosí is not a fixed number: it's driven by size, whether it's new or recycled, the type and quality of the lumber, load capacity, HT treatment and the volume you buy. That's why every quote is calculated around your specific operation.

Search for "pallet prices" and you'll find wildly different figures. That's normal: each listing describes a different product. A light-duty pallet for occasional use doesn't cost the same as a reinforced heavy-duty one, and a recycled pallet doesn't cost the same as a new, heat-treated export pallet. Understanding the factors lets you compare apples to apples and quote with confidence. At BestPack Solutions, a pallet manufacturer with a production plant in San Luis Potosí and a distribution warehouse in Saltillo, here's how the price comes together.

Why is there no single price for a pallet?

Because a pallet is an engineered product, not a fixed catalog item. The price moves primarily on six levers: size, condition (new or recycled), lumber type and quality, load capacity, treatment and volume. Change a single variable and the unit cost changes. Any isolated price you find online describes one specific configuration that may not match what your operation needs.

How do size and design affect the price?

Size is the first factor: the bigger the pallet, the more lumber and the higher the cost. Standard sizes are more affordable because they're produced in volume; custom sizes or special designs cost more. Some common references in Mexico:

  • 1.20 × 1.00 m (40 × 48 inches): the most widely used size for domestic and export operations.
  • 1.20 × 0.80 m (Euro-type / EUR pallet): common on lines built to the European standard.
  • 1.20 × 1.20 m: for wide loads or bulky products.

Design matters too: the number of deck boards, the blocks or stringers, the entry type (two-way or four-way for pallet jacks and forklifts) and whether it carries reinforcements. More pieces and more assembly mean higher cost, but also more strength.

New or recycled pallet: which is better on price?

This is the biggest difference in cost. A recycled pallet is usually the more economical option because it reuses existing lumber and cuts material; a new pallet costs more but delivers consistent uniformity, appearance and load capacity, piece after piece.

With recycled pallets, the price depends on the quality grade: Grade A is nearly equivalent to a new pallet, with minimal repair; Grade B shows visible repairs but keeps its structural function; and Grade C shows more wear and is reserved for less demanding uses. The higher the grade, the higher the price. To dig into when each one makes sense, read our guide to new vs. recycled pallets.

Rule of thumb: for in-plant handling with high turnover, a good-grade recycled pallet performs well and lowers your cost. For export, or when presentation matters, a new heat-treated pallet usually pays off.

Do lumber type and quality change the price?

Yes. Not all lumber is worth the same: denser, stronger species cost more, while treated pine is more affordable. Beyond the species, quality is key: moisture content, board selection (no loose knots or splits) and consistent dimensioning affect both cost and performance. Better-selected, properly dried lumber costs more, but it reduces breakage, losses and claims across your supply chain.

How much does load capacity matter?

Load is the second most decisive variable. A light-duty pallet is not the same as a reinforced one built for heavy or constant loads: the more demanding the use, the more reinforcement in design, pieces and assembly, and therefore the higher the price. As a reference, a standard 1.20 × 1.00 m wooden pallet typically handles on the order of 1,000 kg of dynamic load (in motion) and around 1,500 kg of static load (stacked or at rest); exact values vary with the lumber and the design. Defining your real load correctly avoids overpaying for an over-engineered pallet, or paying for failures with an under-built one.

Why does heat treatment (HT / ISPM 15) add to the cost?

If your goods cross a border, the pallet must be heat-treated (HT) in compliance with the ISPM 15 standard and Mexico's NOM-144, and carry the IPPC stamp. That process — controlled kiln, drying and certification — adds cost, but it's essential: a pallet without it can be held or rejected at customs. For domestic use with no export, it isn't always required, which allows a lower price. Check out our export pallets if this is your case.

Do volume and logistics move the price?

A lot, and it's often overlooked. In industrial purchasing, unit cost drops as volume rises: ordering 50 pallets is not the same as running a program of thousands per month. Delivery frequency (one-time purchase vs. scheduled deliveries) and the destination region also come into play. That's why it pays to state your estimated monthly consumption when you request a quote: with current production of more than 150,000 pallets per month and installed capacity of up to 350,000, BestPack can sustain high volumes with steady deliveries in San Luis Potosí and nationwide across Mexico, which stabilizes your cost.

How do I request an accurate quote?

To receive a real price rather than a generic estimate, share these five details:

  • Pallet size (for example, 1.20 × 1.00 m).
  • Condition: new or recycled (and, if recycled, the grade you're looking for).
  • Load it will carry and type of use (light, standard or heavy-duty).
  • Treatment: whether it needs HT for export or not.
